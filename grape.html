GRAPE: Optimize Data Mixture for Group Robust Multi-target Adaptive PrEtraining

Multi-Source-Multi-Target Domain Reweighting via Group DRO

Simin Fan, Maria Ios Glarou, Martin Jaggi • MLO, EPFL • NeurIPS 2025

Paper Code

Abstract

▼
The performance of large language models (LLMs) across diverse downstream applications is fundamentally governed by the quality and composition of their pretraining corpora. Existing domain reweighting algorithms primarily optimize data mixtures for a single target task, thereby resulting in models that overfit to specialized objectives while exhibiting substantial performance degradation on other benchmarks. This paper introduces Group Robust Multi-target Adaptive Pretraining (GRAPE), a novel multi-source-multi-target domain reweighting framework designed to calibrate pretraining data mixtures for robust performance across multiple target tasks simultaneously. GRAPE dynamically adjusts sampling weights across source domains (domain weights) while concurrently modulating task weights that quantify the relative importance of each individual target task. This adaptive process prioritizes tasks based on their learning difficulty throughout training. We formulate this interleaved reweighting mechanism as a minimax optimization problem: The inner maximization adjusts task weights leveraging group distributed-robust-optimization (DRO), where those tasks demonstrating the least improvement under the current data mixture are prioritized with higher weights; The outer minimization then optimizes domain weights to maximize loss reduction on the prioritized tasks. Experiments on ClimbLab dataset demonstrate that GRAPE consistently outperforms baseline methods in terms of reasoning performance across 6 benchmarks. Furthermore, when applied to multilingual targets, GRAPE effectively identifies optimal training mixtures from mainstream languages, achieving superior language modeling capabilities across 8 low-resource target languages.

Method

GRAPE is a multi-source–multi-target domain reweighting algorithm. It runs on top of standard pretraining and learns task weights $\textcolor{#9CAF88}{\mathbf{z}}$ over target tasks and domain weights $\textcolor{#B85450}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}$ over pretraining domains, so that the data mixture is always steered toward the tasks that are currently learning slowest. This approach is guided by a key principle:

Tasks that are improving slowly deserve more attention.

GRAPE interleaves three operations during training:

1. Standard Pretraining (every step)

At each training step, sample a batch from $\mathcal{D}_{\text{train}}$ according to current domain weights $\textcolor{#B85450}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}$, compute the loss, and update model parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ via gradient descent.

2. Task Reweighting (every $\Delta T_{\mathbf{z}}$ steps)

GRAPE identifies tasks that are learning slowly and assigns them higher task weights via Group DRO, ensuring they receive more attention during training. To measure learning speed, GRAPE uses each task's Rate-of-Improvement (RoI):

$$r_n^{(t)} := \frac{\textcolor{#9CAF88}{l_n(\boldsymbol{\theta}_t) - l_n(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1})}}{\textcolor{#64748B}{l_n(\boldsymbol{\theta}_t)}}$$
→
Loss improvement: Numerator captures how much task $n$'s loss decreased in one step
→
Normalization: Denominator normalizes by current loss, making RoI scale-invariant across tasks
→
RoI: Relative improvement rate—the normalized rate at which task $n$'s loss decreases

Using a first-order Taylor expansion$^{*}$, RoI can be expressed as:

$$r_n^{(t)} \approx \gamma_t \sum_{k=1}^K \textcolor{#B85450}{\alpha_k^t} \textcolor{#6B7280}{\langle \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \log l_n(\boldsymbol{\theta}_t), \mathbf{g}_k(\boldsymbol{\theta}_t) \rangle}$$
α
Domain weights: Current data mixture proportions $\textcolor{#B85450}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}^t}$ determine how much each domain contributes
⟨⟩
Inner product: Measures gradient alignment—how well domain $k$'s gradient direction aligns with task $n$'s improvement direction
Σ
Weighted sum: Quantifies the expected improvement for task $n$ under the current domain mixture (low values signal ineffective mixtures).

$^{*}$ Taylor Expansion: For small learning rates $\gamma_t$, we apply a first-order Taylor expansion to express the loss change:

$$l_n(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{t+1}) \approx l_n(\boldsymbol{\theta}_t) - \textcolor{#B85450}{\gamma_t \sum_{k=1}^K \alpha_k^t \mathbf{g}_k(\boldsymbol{\theta}_t)} \cdot \textcolor{#9CAF88}{\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} l_n(\boldsymbol{\theta}_t)}$$

Substituting into the RoI definition and simplifying yields the gradient alignment form shown above. The parameter update combines domain gradients weighted by $\boldsymbol{\alpha}$, while the gradient term captures the first-order improvement direction.

3. Domain Reweighting (every $\Delta T_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}$ steps)

Once task weights $\textcolor{#9CAF88}{\mathbf{z}}$ identify which tasks need help, GRAPE updates domain weights $\textcolor{#B85450}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}$ to favor domains whose gradients align well with these prioritized tasks. This steers the data mixture toward domains that benefit the lagging tasks.

Together, this forms a minimax Group DRO game: task weights $\textcolor{#9CAF88}{\mathbf{z}}$ focus on the slowest-improving tasks, while domain weights $\textcolor{#B85450}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}$ maximize improvement on those tasks.

Update Rules

GRAPE uses multiplicative update rules derived from the minimax Group DRO objective:

Task weights $\textcolor{#9CAF88}{\mathbf{z}}$: Increase for tasks with low RoI (slow improvement)

$$\textcolor{#9CAF88}{\mathbf{z}^{t+1}} \propto \textcolor{#9CAF88}{\mathbf{z}^t} \odot \exp\bigg( - \frac{\gamma_t }{\mu_{\mathbf{z}}} \textcolor{#6B7280}{\langle \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \log l_n(\boldsymbol{\theta}_t), \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \ell(\boldsymbol{\theta}_t; \mathbf{x}) \rangle} \bigg)$$

The inner product measures alignment between task gradients and training gradients. Low alignment → higher task weight.

Domain weights $\textcolor{#B85450}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}$: Increase for domains that align with high-weight tasks

$$\textcolor{#B85450}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{t+1}} \propto \textcolor{#B85450}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}^t} \odot \exp\bigg( \frac{\gamma_t}{\mu_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}} \textcolor{#6B7280}{\langle \mathbf{g}_k(\boldsymbol{\theta}_t), \nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}} \log \ell(\boldsymbol{\theta}_t; \mathbf{y}) \rangle} \bigg)$$

The inner product measures alignment between domain gradients and task-weighted gradients. High alignment → higher domain weight.

Ablation Studies

We conduct comprehensive ablation studies to assess GRAPE's design choices and robustness. Click on each study to expand details.

1 Progress Assessment Metrics for Group DRO

▼

We compare three progress metrics for Group DRO: Rate-of-Improvement (RoI) — GRAPE's choice, Gap-of-Improvement (GoI), and EMA-Rate-of-Improvement (RoI-ema):

Gap-of-Improvement (GoI)
$$GoI_{n}^{(t)} := l_n(\theta_t) - l_n(\theta_{t+1})$$

Uses absolute improvement without normalization.

EMA-Rate-of-Improvement (RoI-ema)
$$RoI\text{-}ema_{n}^{(t)} := \frac{l_n(\theta_t) - l_n(\theta_{t+1})}{l^t_{ema,n}}$$

Normalizes by exponential moving average: $l_{ema,n}^t = \beta \cdot l_{ema,n}^{t-1} + (1-\beta)\cdot l_n(\theta_t)$ where $\beta = 0.7$.

Rate-of-Improvement (RoI) — GRAPE
$$r_{n}^{(t)} := \frac{l_n(\theta_t) - l_n(\theta_{t+1})}{l_n(\theta_t)}$$

Normalizes by current loss, providing scale-invariant relative progress.

Experimental Results

Evaluated on 12 diverse task combinations (2-8 tasks) on SlimPajama. In average log-perplexity: GRAPE with RoI wins 7, GRAPE-gap (GoI) wins 3, and GRAPE-ema (RoI-ema) wins 2. All GRAPE variants outperform Uniform and DoGE on all 12 task combinations.

Average Log-Perplexity: GRAPE (RoI) achieves best performance on 7 out of 12 task sets.
Worst-Case Log-Perplexity: GRAPE (RoI) demonstrates superior robustness, minimizing worst-case performance across tasks.
Key Findings
GoI (GRAPE-gap)

Problem: Prioritizes intrinsically easier tasks with larger loss values, biasing attention away from tasks that genuinely need help. Tasks with larger loss values can exhibit larger absolute improvements even when their relative progress is slow.

RoI-ema (GRAPE-ema)

Problem: Historical dependency from exponential moving average may slow adaptation. Smoothed historical loss might not reflect current learning state, leading to delayed adjustments.

RoI (GRAPE)

Advantage: Scale-invariant normalization by current loss mitigates bias toward tasks with higher loss magnitudes. Offers fair assessment and captures both magnitude and direction of improvement.

5 Hyperparameter Sensitivity

▼

We perform hyperparameter search on reweighting intervals $\Delta T_{\alpha}$ and $\Delta T_z$ to assess sensitivity and efficiency trade-offs.

Update Frequency: 50 steps

Most frequent updates don't yield best performance—RoI metric becomes more reliable after sufficient training steps. Higher computational overhead.

Update Frequency: 100 steps

Optimal balance between performance and efficiency. GRAPE consistently outperforms DoGE while maintaining reasonable computational overhead (~15% for SlimPajama with $K=7$, $N=6$).

Update Frequency: 200 steps

Less frequent updates reduce overhead but may miss rapid changes in task priorities. GRAPE still outperforms DoGE, demonstrating robustness to update frequency.

Key Insight: GRAPE consistently outperforms DoGE across all tested update frequencies. The optimal frequency (100 steps) balances responsiveness with reliability of the RoI metric.

Limitations

  • Hyperparameter sensitivity: Performance depends on regularization coefficients and update frequencies, which require tuning for optimal results.
  • Data quality and domain granularity: Effectiveness is influenced by pretraining corpus quality and domain partition granularity—fine-grained semantic clustering yields better results than coarse partitions.
  • Computational overhead: The reweighting mechanism introduces computational overhead of approximately $(N+1)\Delta T_z^{-1} + (K+1)\Delta T_\alpha^{-1}$ compared to standard pretraining, where $K$ is the number of source domains and $N$ is the number of target tasks.

Future Work

  • Scaling effects: Understanding how optimal learning curricula vary with model scale and data quality.
  • Sample-level DRO: Extending the framework to prioritize individual hard examples rather than entire tasks.
  • Theoretical step-sizes: Deriving near-optimal update frequencies to reduce hyperparameter tuning.
